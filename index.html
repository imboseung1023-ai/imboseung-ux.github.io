2026 Nuclear Innovation Challenge · Controls and instrumentation

Self-Checking Reactor Controller Simulated reactor control plus a dye concentration rig

A reactor power controller that catches its own drifting sensor, tested in simulation and paired with a bench rig that doses dye and measures concentration optically.

  1. The problem

    The baseline PID controller trusts one power sensor completely. If that sensor drifts, the controller follows it and moves the real reactor away from its target, and nothing in the loop notices.

  2. Sensor cross-check

    Fuel runs hotter than coolant in proportion to power, so a heat balance on the temperature sensors gives a second, independent power measurement. The controller tracks the slow difference between neutron and thermal power and corrects for it, capped at 10%.

  3. Retune and scoring tool

    We added a two-second filter on the power signal and raised the gain from 3e-4 to 1e-3. A Python scoring tool runs every controller through eight scenarios and grades tracking, rod movement and time in alarm states.

  4. Results

    Overall score improved from 2.28 to 2.10 (lower is better). On the drifting sensor, tracking error fell from 1.89 to 0.34 and rod range used dropped from 90% to about 15%. Setpoint changes settle about 20 s faster.

  5. The dye rig

    A bench analogue for boron injection: pumps dose dye into a stirred tank and a TCS3200 optical sensor infers concentration by Beer-Lambert, A = ε · l · c. A well-mixed mass balance gives the dosing time, with τ = V / Q = 350 mL / 7 mL/s = 50 s.

  6. Limits

    The calibration is only linear at low dose: absorbance flattens near 0.6 to 0.7 after three to four drops. The tank model assumes perfect mixing, and this is a teaching model, not licensing-grade analysis.

Reaction chemistry · ChE 180 Design Studio · Soap Symposium

Fresh Brew'd Soap Coffee soap by saponification

A coffee-themed bar soap my team of 4 formulated, made and presented, using real brewed coffee and coffee grounds as the exfoliant.

  1. Formulation

    Five oils: coconut, shea butter, linseed, rice bran and castor. We balanced each between 15% and 25% of the blend, with rice bran oil highest at 25% and coconut at 23%, inside a 500 g batch that includes 190 g of water.

  2. Soap calculator

    We built a soap calculator in class and cross-checked it against an online one, then used 95% of the calculated NaOH. Stoichiometry and material balances kept every batch in a safe pH range of 8 to 10.

  3. Modified cold process

    We brewed the coffee first and dissolved the lye into it, melted the solid oils and fats to speed things up, then combined everything and let it saponify.

  4. Result

    60 g bars with hardness, cleansing, conditioning and lather close to our targets, and conditioning in the high range. Coffee grounds from the V1 cafeteria give a mild scrub.

  5. Symposium

    We presented as Group 27 with a poster and a hand-washing station where visitors tried the soaps. The bars are wrapped in compostable unbleached parchment and priced at $3 each.

Engineering hackathon · Robotics prototype

PLIC Power Line Ice Cleaner

A small robot that rides along a power line and clears ice before its weight can bring the line down.

  1. The problem

    Freezing rain and snow load power lines with ice. The added weight damages or snaps them, and the outage that follows cuts heat, elevators, traffic signals and medical devices. People with disabilities and mobility challenges are hit hardest.

  2. What exists today

    Drones, helicopters, climbing crews and heated lines. They are expensive, risky in bad weather, and mostly corrective: they only start after the ice has formed.

  3. Brainstorm

    We compared heated cables, mechanical scraping, rotating cleaning wheels and vibration, and worked through the heat transfer math on paper before choosing a mechanical crawler.

  4. The design

    Two motor-driven wheels grip the line inside a light frame. The electronics sit at the bottom to keep the centre of mass low, so the robot stays stable on the wire, and line guides reduce error from wind.

  5. What's next

    A 3D-printed frame, power harvested from the line's magnetic field plus solar panels, and an ultrasonic sensor in place of the bumper switch to detect the end of the line.

Mechatronics · Brock University

Marble Sorter Autonomous colour sorting and counting

A fully autonomous machine that sorts balls by colour and reports the final count of each. I led the team of 4 that designed and built it.

  1. The goal

    Take a mixed batch of balls, sort them by colour with no human input, and determine the final count of each colour.

  2. Concepts

    We started with sketches and proofs of concept: a pressure detector, a distance detector and a ball cage. Those turned into test parts before we committed to a full design.

  3. Construction

    We built one design concept as a test, then implemented it in the full machine with sensors and motor-servo actuators on a robotics kit.

  4. Result

    98% sorting accuracy at 120 marbles per hour, which beat our performance target by 25%.

  5. What I'd fix

    The holding component at the start isn't secured well, and some connectors sit together instead of actually restricting movement. A rebuild would lock those joints properly.
